## 1. The "Injaesang" (Talent Image) Disconnect

In the West, a resume is a list of individual achievements. In Korea, it is a testament to how well you fit a specific **"Injaesang" (인재상)**—the idealized "talent image" of a company. 

When you translate "I led a team to increase sales by 20%," you are highlighting individual capability. While important, a Korean recruiter is looking for keywords like **'Seongsil' (성실 - sincerity/diligence)** and **'Hyup-eop' (협업 - collaboration)**. 

A "flawless" translation often misses these nuances. For instance, if you are applying for a "Generalist" track at a conglomerate (Chaebol), emphasizing your niche expertise too heavily can actually backfire. They want to see that you are a "harmonious" person who can endure the rigors of the hierarchy. If your resume doesn't use the specific vocabulary found in that company’s 2026 vision statement, it shows a lack of "effort" (Jeong-seong), which is a fatal flaw in Korean hiring.

## 2. The 'Jagisogaeseo' Trap: It’s Not a Cover Letter

Many foreign applicants treat the **Jagisogaeseo (Self-Introduction Letter)** as a simple cover letter. This is a massive mistake. The Jagisogaeseo is a structured psychological profile. 

One of the most common sections is the **"Growth Process" (성장과정)**. We often see foreign candidates write about their childhood hobbies. To a Korean HR manager, this section is meant to prove your **consistency**. They want to see how your environment shaped your work ethic. 

- **The Foreign Approach**: "I grew up in London and enjoyed sports, which taught me teamwork." (Too generic, sounds like a student).
- **The ApplyGoGo Re-engineered Approach**: "Growing up in a multicultural environment in London, I developed 'Adaptability' (Jeog-eung-ryeok). I demonstrated this by leading a diverse team during X project, achieving Y results through 'Grit' (Kkeun-gi)."

We translate your "Western" confidence into "Korean" humility—a specific tone that conveys high competence without sounding individualistic.

## 3. Formatting is the First Interview

If you send a standard 1-page PDF resume to a traditional Korean firm, you’ve already lost. Despite the rise of "Blind Recruitment," many firms still prefer specific formats—often involving chronological education history starting from high school, precise dates for every certification, and a very specific level of detail regarding military service (if applicable) or gaps in employment.

Furthermore, the "vibe" of the language matters. Using formal **'Hapsyo-che' (합쇼체)** honorifics is non-negotiable. If your translation uses 'Haeyo-che' (informal polite), you sound like a tourist, not a professional.
