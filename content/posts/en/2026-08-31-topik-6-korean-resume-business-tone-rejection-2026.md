## 1. The 'Professional Nuance Gap': Why TOPIK 6 Isn't Enough

The most common mistake foreign experts make is assuming that "Fluent Korean" equals "Business Korean." It does not. 

In the Korean corporate world, there is a specific linguistic register known as *Gong-mun-seo* (공문서)—the language of official documents. Most foreign applicants, even those with TOPIK 6, write their *Jagisogaeseo* (Self-introduction letter) in a style that we call "Translation-ese." It is grammatically correct but culturally hollow.

When a Korean HR manager reads a resume translated by a generic agency or AI, they immediately notice a lack of **Business Maturity**. Using simple honorifics (*Jondaetmal*) is the bare minimum. To win an offer, you must use industry-specific terminology and sentence structures that demonstrate *Gyo-yang* (refinement) and *Sacheon-eup* (corporate etiquette).

## 2. The "Hard-Working" Fallacy: Keywords That Kill Your Chances

Many international candidates try to impress Korean recruiters by emphasizing how "passionate" or "hard-working" they are. In the West, these are great buzzwords. In Korea, they are "empty words."

In the competitive Korean market, "passion" is expected. It is the baseline. If you want to stand out, you must pivot to **'Seongsil' (성실 - Sincerity/Integrity)** and **'Chigim-gam' (책임감 - Sense of Responsibility)**. But here’s the kicker: you cannot just say these words. You must prove them through a very specific Korean narrative structure.

Korean HR managers look for the "Growth Process" (*Seongjang Gwangjeong*). They don't just want to see your wins; they want to see how you handled a crisis within a team hierarchy. If your resume focuses 100% on individual achievements without showing **"Organizational Harmony,"** it will be flagged as "Not a fit for Korean culture."

## 3. Formatting: The HWP and PDF Barrier

If you are still sending a standard 1-page American-style resume to a traditional Korean firm, you are already behind. 

The Korean *Iryeok-seo* (Resume) has a very rigid structure. It often requires your education history in a specific chronological order (starting from high school), certifications, and sometimes even specific formatting that only looks right in **Hancom (HWP)** files. 

While tech companies like Kakao are more flexible, the "hidden" expectation of a professional layout still exists. A resume that "looks foreign" triggers a subconscious bias in HR managers: *“Will this person be able to communicate effectively with our senior directors who only use HWP and formal templates?”*
