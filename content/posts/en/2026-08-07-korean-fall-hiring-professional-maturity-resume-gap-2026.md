## 1. The "1-Page" Trap: Efficiency vs. Sincerity

In North America and Europe, a two-page resume is often considered "too long." In Korea, a one-page resume for a mid-career professional is often viewed as "empty." 

Korean HR managers equate the effort put into a document with the candidate's desire for the role. When they see a minimalist Western CV, they don't see an efficient communicator; they see someone who did a "copy-paste" job and didn't care enough to adapt to Korean norms. 

This is the **Professional Maturity Gap**. Mature candidates in the Korean market demonstrate their fit through the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* (Detailed Career Statement), a document that essentially doesn't exist in the Western playbook. While your CV says *what* you did, the Korean recruiter wants to see the *how*, the *why*, and the *with whom*.

## 2. The Missing Piece: Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo (Detailed Career Statement)

If you are applying for an experienced role (Gyeongnyeok-jik), a standard resume is only 30% of the battle. The remaining 70% is the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo*. 

This document requires you to break down your projects into a specific hierarchy:
1.  **Project Period & Goal**: Exactly when and what was the objective?
2.  **Your Specific Role**: Not just "Team Lead," but your specific scope of authority.
3.  **Key Actions (The 'How')**: In Korea, the process is as important as the result. Did you use *Seongsil* (sincerity) to overcome a hurdle?
4.  **Quantitative & Qualitative Results**: How did this benefit the organization?

Without this detailed breakdown, Korean recruiters perceive your profile as "thin." They suspect you might be hiding a lack of depth behind shiny English buzzwords. To pass the August screening, you must transform your bullet points into a narrative of professional growth.

## 3. The "Culture Code" in Keywords

In the West, we use aggressive verbs: *Spearheaded, Overhauled, Orchestrated.* 
In Korea, while results are vital, the "Collective Harmony" and "Diligence" codes are equally important. Use terms like:
*   **Seongsil (Sincerity/Diligence)**: Proving you are a reliable "brick" in the corporate foundation.
*   **Hyup-up (Collaboration)**: Showing you aren't just a "lone wolf" high-performer, but someone who elevates the team.
*   **Responsibility (Chaegim-gam)**: Demonstrating that you stay until the job is done.

A resume that only highlights "I did X" without mentioning "I contributed to the team's Y" is a red flag for Korean conglomerates. They aren't just hiring a skill set; they are hiring a future colleague who will fit into their specific corporate hierarchy.

## 4. Why "Doing it Yourself" is a High-Stakes Gamble

Many candidates attempt to bridge this gap using Google Translate or basic AI tools. This is a fatal mistake. 
1.  **Honorifics & Tone**: A slight error in *Jondaemal* (polite speech) in your *Jagisogaeseo* (Self-Introduction) can make you look arrogant or uneducated.
2.  **Formatting Etiquette**: Korean resumes often require specific layouts—sometimes even in HWP format—that follow a chronological order starting from high school. Missing these "minor" details signals a lack of cultural readiness.
3.  **The "English-Only" Bubble**: Even if the job description is in English, the HR person screening it is likely a Korean native who values traditional document structures.
