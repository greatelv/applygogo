## 1. The "Impact" Fallacy: Why Western Bullets Fail in Korea

In the West, recruiters want "Impact." They want to see: *"Increased revenue by 20% through X strategy."* While data is important in Korea, it is secondary to the **context of responsibility.**

Korean HR managers in 2026 are looking for what we call the 'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo' (경력기술서) — a Detailed Career Description. Unlike a Western resume that focuses on the *result*, a Korean career description focuses on the *hierarchy of the task and your place within the organizational structure.*

If you simply list achievements, a Korean recruiter sees an "Individualist." They don't see how you followed instructions, how you collaborated within the specific Korean seniority system, or the "Sincerity" (*Seongsil*) you applied to mundane but essential tasks. In 2026, with the rise of AI-driven initial screenings, if your resume lacks the specific structural flow of **Task -> Role -> Action -> Result -> Learning**, it will be filtered out as "insincere" or "lacking detail."

## 2. The 'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo' vs. The CV

Most foreigners make the mistake of thinking a *Jagisogaeseo* (Cover Letter/Self-Introduction) is the only extra document they need. They are wrong.

For the 2026 Fall wave, the **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo** is the true battlefield. This document requires a narrative flow that English CVs simply lack. It is often 2-3 pages long, meticulously organized by project, and uses a specific professional vernacular that balances humility with authority.

Key elements you are likely missing:
*   **Organizational Alignment:** You must demonstrate not just what you did, but how it aligned with the department's yearly goals.
*   **The 'Growth Process' Logic:** Even in technical resumes, Korean companies want to see a trajectory of "loyalty" and "perseverance."
*   **The HWP/Word Hybrid Formatting:** While PDFs are becoming more common, the structural expectation of specific boxes, tables, and chronological order (starting from your high school graduation in some cases) remains a psychological barrier for Korean recruiters when they see a "free-form" Western resume.

## 3. The Dangerous Trap of "Manual Translation"

"I'll just translate my English resume using DeepL or ChatGPT," you might think. This is the fastest way to a rejection.

Korean is a language of **nuance and honorifics.** A resume written in "Standard" Korean often sounds robotic or, worse, unintentionally rude. Professional Korean requires a specific level of *Hapsho-che* (formal polite) ending that communicates a specific type of professional "Sincerity" (*Seongsil*). 

Furthermore, Western professional terms don't always have a 1:1 translation. Translating "Growth Hacker" literally into Korean can make you sound like a computer criminal to an older HR director. You need **localization**, not translation. You need your career "re-engineered" to fit the cultural expectations of a 50-year-old Korean department head who values stability as much as innovation.
