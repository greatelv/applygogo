## 1. The October Shift: From 'Chae-yong' to 'Retention'

In the Korean recruitment calendar, October marks a critical transition. The massive, standardized hiring waves (*Gong-chae*) of conglomerates like Samsung and Hyundai are winding down. The spotlight shifts to mid-sized firms (*Jung-gyeon gieop*) and specialized tech startups. 

Unlike the giants, these companies don't have the luxury of "hiring for potential" and letting you figure it out. They are looking for someone to plug a gap **permanently**.

When a Korean HR manager at a mid-sized firm sees a Western-style resume highlighting a new job every two years and a series of "individual hero moments," they don't see a high-performer. They see a **"Flight Risk" (Twae-sa wi-heom).** They see a foreigner who will likely leave the moment a slightly better offer comes along or when they get bored of the local corporate culture. In October, you aren't being tested on your talent; you are being tested on your **stability.**

## 2. The Narrative Gap: 'What' vs. 'How'

Western resumes are obsessed with **"What"**: *What did you achieve? What was the ROI? What tools did you use?*

Korean recruiters, particularly during the "Stability Test" phase, are obsessed with **"How"**: *How did you handle conflict with your senior? How did you adapt to a rigid deadline? How did you contribute to the 'Hoesik' (team culture) without compromising productivity?*

This is where the **Jagisogaeseo (Self-Introduction Letter)** becomes your greatest hurdle. Most foreigners treat the Jagisogaeseo as a mere cover letter. It isn't. It is a psychological profile. 

If your self-intro doesn't use keywords like **'Seongsil' (Sincerity)**, **'Innae' (Perseverance)**, or **'Hyub-up' (Cooperation)**, you are effectively telling the recruiter that you are a lone wolf. In a society that prizes *Woori* (Us) over *Na* (Me), a lone wolf is a liability, not an asset.

## 3. The 'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo' vs. The Bullet Point

A standard English CV uses punchy bullet points. A Korean **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo (Career Description)** requires a structured, narrative-driven breakdown of your professional history. 

Recruiters want to see the specific hierarchy of your previous teams. They want to see the *reason* for your job changes described with professional grace. If you simply list "Left for better opportunity," you’ve failed. If you don't provide a detailed technical narrative of your projects in the specific HWP or PDF format they expect, your resume won't even be read—it will be filtered out by the ATS (Applicant Tracking System) or dismissed for "lack of effort."

## 4. The "Translation" Trap

Many candidates think, "I'll just run my resume through ChatGPT or Google Translate." 

**This is a fatal mistake.** 

Korean is a language of hierarchies. Using the wrong honorific level (*Jondaemal*) in your resume makes you look uneducated or arrogant. Conversely, being too humble makes you look incompetent. There is a precise, "corporate-neutral" tone that only native-level experts (or highly specialized AI) can achieve. 

Korean HR managers can smell a machine-translated resume from the first sentence. To them, an unlocalized resume signals that you aren't serious about living in Korea. If you can’t bother to localize your resume, why would they believe you’ll bother to learn the company’s internal culture?
