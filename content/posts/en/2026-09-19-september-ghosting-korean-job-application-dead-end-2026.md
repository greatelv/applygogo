## 1. The Fatal Flaw: The 1-Page Resume Myth

In the US or Europe, a one-page resume is the gold standard. It’s a highlight reel of your achievements. In Korea, a one-page resume is often viewed as "incomplete" or "lazy." 

Korean HR managers aren't just looking for your achievements; they are looking for a forensic breakdown of your professional history. This is where the *Gyeongnyeok-kisulseo* (Detailed Career Description) comes in. While your *Jagisogaeseo* (Self-Introduction) covers your personality and fit, the *Gyeongnyeok-kisulseo* is the technical backbone of your application.

Without it, even if you are the most qualified candidate from a Fortune 500 company, you are a "ghost" in the system. The HR manager simply doesn't have the specific data points they need to "score" your application against their internal rubric.

## 2. Anatomy of a Winning 'Gyeongnyeok-kisulseo'

A Western resume might say: *"Led a team of 5 to increase sales by 20%."* 

A Korean HR manager reading a *Gyeongnyeok-kisulseo* expects to see:
*   **Project Period**: Exactly how many months and years.
*   **Specific Role**: Were you the PM, the Lead Dev, or a Contributor?
*   **Tech Stack/Tools**: Every single software, methodology (Agile, Scrum), and tool used.
*   **Contribution Percentage**: How much of the final result was *your* specific doing?
*   **Key Results (with Korean Context)**: How does this apply to the Korean market or the specific challenges the Korean firm is facing?

If you are applying to a conglomerate like Samsung or a "Next-Unicorn" startup, they need to see "Seongsil" (sincerity/diligence) through the depth of your documentation. If your application looks physically "thin" compared to a local candidate’s 5-page dossier, you lose by default.

## 3. The "English-Friendly" Trap

Many foreigners fall into the trap of thinking "Global Team = Global Standards." 

While the team might speak English, the **HR Department (Insa-tim)** is usually 100% Korean. They are the gatekeepers. They use standardized scoring systems designed for Korean resumes. When they receive a Western CV, they have to manually translate your experience into their internal format to get approval from higher-ups. 

**Most HR managers won't do that work for you.** If your resume isn't already "pre-digested" into the format they recognize, it goes straight to the digital trash bin. This is why you are being ghosted in late September—you are making the recruiter work too hard.

## 4. Why Google Translate is Your Career’s Worst Enemy

You might think, "I'll just translate my resume into Korean using AI or Google Translate." 

**Do not do this.** 

Korean business language is tiered by levels of politeness and specific professional jargon (*Hanja*-based terms). A Google-translated resume often uses "Ban-mal" (informal) or awkward "Jondaet-mal" (formal) that sounds like a textbook from the 1990s. To a Korean recruiter, this doesn't just show a lack of language skills; it shows a lack of **cultural intelligence (CQ)**. 

They wonder: *"If this person can't even get their resume tone right, how will they communicate with our senior directors (Sang-mu-nim)?"*
