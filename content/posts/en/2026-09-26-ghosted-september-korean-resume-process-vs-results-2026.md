In the West, we are taught to sell the *What*. In Korea, HR managers are obsessed with the *How*. If you don't bridge this gap, your global results look like "lone wolf" exaggerations rather than evidence of organizational fit.

## 1. The 'Lone Wolf' vs. 'The Cog': Why Results Alone Scare Korean HR

In Western corporate culture, the individual is the hero. Your resume likely uses verbs like "Led," "Spearheaded," and "Owned." You focus on the final number. To a Korean recruiter, particularly those in traditional conglomerates (Chaebols), a resume that only lists high-level results without detailing the collaborative friction feels "empty" or, worse, arrogant.

Korean recruitment operates on the principle of **Seongsil (Sincerity)**. They want to see the "Growth Process." They want to know:
- Who did you report to during this project?
- How did you handle the conflict with the cross-functional team?
- What specific steps did you take to ensure the process was repeatable?

When you submit a concise, bulleted Western resume, the Korean recruiter sees a lack of effort. They see someone who might not respect the "Step-by-Step" hierarchy that defines Korean work culture.

## 2. The Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo Logic: Moving Beyond the Bullet Point

In Korea, the resume is often split into two parts: the *Iryeokseo* (Basic Info) and the **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo** (Detailed Experience Description). 

The *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* is where most foreign candidates fail. While you might write: 
> *"Increased user engagement by 15% through a new UI rollout."*

A winning Korean-style narrative would look more like:
> *"Identified a 10% drop in user retention during the Q1 audit. Collaborated with the 5-member design team over a 3-month sprint. Conducted 20 user interviews to align with the 'Customer First' vision. Successfully localized the UI for the Korean market, resulting in a 15% engagement increase and receiving the 'Team Excellence' commendation."*

Notice the difference? The second version proves you can navigate a team, follow a timeline, and align with corporate values. It shows **integration**. Without this "Process-heavy" narrative, your achievements lack the structural sincerity required to prove you aren't just a "talent mercenary" who will quit in six months.

## 3. The Formatting "Smell Test"

If you are sending a .docx or .pdf file in a standard Harvard/Google format, you are already at a disadvantage. While tech companies like Coupang or Toss are more flexible, the vast majority of Korean firms still have a "smell test" for resumes.

- **The Photo Trap**: In many Western countries, photos are a no-go for anti-discrimination reasons. In Korea, while "Blind Recruitment" is growing, a professional, polished headshot is often still the unspoken standard. 
- **The Education Hierarchy**: Korean resumes list education from high school onwards. Skipping your high school or early university details can make your history look "incomplete" to a recruiter used to seeing a full life-arc.
- **The Language Nuance**: Using Google Translate to turn your English resume into Korean is a death sentence. Mistaken honorifics (using *Banmal* logic in a professional doc) or using awkward direct translations for technical terms signals that you will be a "communication burden" for the team.
