## 1. The Seniority Gap: Why "Impact" Isn't Enough

In Western markets, a resume is an achievement-oriented marketing document. You focus on **impact**: "Increased revenue by 20%," or "Optimized workflow efficiency." You sell the *result*.

However, the Korean corporate mindset—even at modern tech giants like Kakao or Coupang—is still deeply rooted in a structured hierarchy. Korean HR managers aren't just looking for what you did; they are looking for where you fit in the **"Gyegeup" (Rank)** system. 

In Korea, career progression follows a strict internal logic: *Sawon* (Associate), *Daeri* (Assistant Manager), *Gwajang* (Manager), *Cha-jang* (Deputy General Manager), and *Bujang* (General Manager). If your resume doesn't explicitly map your global experience to these specific milestones using professional Korean business terminology, the recruiter defaults to the safest option: **the bottom.**

## 2. The Missing Link: The 'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo'

The biggest mistake foreign applicants make is submitting a standard one-page CV and expecting it to suffice. In Korea, for anyone with more than three years of experience, the **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo (Experience Statement)** is more important than the resume itself.

While a *Jagisogaeseo* (Personal Statement) focuses on your character and growth, the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* is a cold, clinical breakdown of your professional history. It requires:
*   **Detailed Project Scopes**: Exact headcount managed, specific budget figures, and your precise role within the hierarchy.
*   **Standardized Terminology**: Using words like *'Ju-do'* (Led/Spearheaded) vs. *'Cham-yeo'* (Participated) determines your rank.
*   **Hierarchy Mapping**: Translating "Project Lead" into a Korean equivalent that matches the years of experience required for a *Gwajang* or *Chajang* level.

Without this document, localized into high-level "Business Korean," an HR manager cannot verify your seniority level. They cannot risk hiring a "Senior Manager" from overseas who might only have the functional experience of a local "Junior." To mitigate risk, they downgrade your status.

## 3. The "Translation" Trap: Why Google Translate Kills Your Career

Many candidates try to bypass this by translating their English resume into Korean using AI or basic translation services. This is a fatal error. 

Korean is a language of hierarchy. The level of honorifics and the choice of nouns in a professional document signal your own seniority. If your resume uses "casual" professional Korean or incorrect honorifics, you immediately signal a lack of cultural competence. 

More importantly, generic translation doesn't "re-rank" you. It simply translates the words. A "Senior Associate" in a US law firm and a "Senior Associate" in a Korean firm have completely different social and professional weights. If you don't re-engineer the narrative to reflect the **competencies** Korean HR associates with a specific rank, you will remain undervalued.
