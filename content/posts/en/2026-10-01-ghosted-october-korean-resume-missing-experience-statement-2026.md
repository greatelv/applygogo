## 1. The 'One-Page' Myth vs. The Korean 'Process' Requirement

In the US or Europe, a one-page resume is the gold standard. Recruiter attention spans are short, and brevity is seen as a sign of seniority. However, the Korean corporate mindset—from legacy conglomerates like Samsung and Hyundai to modern giants like Kakao and Coupang—operates on a different logic: **The Logic of Sincerity (Seongsil, 성실).**

When a Korean HR manager looks at a 1-page English resume with five bullet points per job, they don’t see "efficient communication." They see a lack of detail. They see someone who hasn't explained the *process*.

In Korea, the **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo (Career Description)** is a separate, multi-page document that breaks down your professional history into granular detail. It’s not just about *what* you did, but *how* you did it, who you collaborated with, what obstacles you faced, and the specific methodology you used to overcome them.

## 2. 'Process and Sincerity': What Your Bullet Points are Missing

I recently reviewed a resume from a Senior Product Manager from Berlin applying for a role in Seoul. His resume said: *"Increased user retention by 20% through A/B testing."* 

In a Western context, that’s a great bullet point. In the Korean context, it's an unfinished sentence. A Korean HR manager wants to know:
1.  What was the specific hypothesis of the A/B test?
2.  Which stakeholders (Design, Engineering, Marketing) did you have to persuade?
3.  How did this align with the company's "Vision 2030"?
4.  What did you do when the first three tests failed?

This is where the concept of **'Process'** comes in. Korean hiring is risk-averse. They want to see your "working style" mapped out. Simply translating your English bullet points into Korean won't fix this. You need to re-engineer your narrative to emphasize persistence and methodological rigor. Without the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo*, you are failing the "Sincerity Test" before you even get an interview.

## 3. The Formatting Trap: HWP, PDF, and the 'Blind' Filter

Even if your content is perfect, your delivery might be getting you filtered out. While the world has moved to PDF, many traditional Korean HR systems—especially those used for "Blind Recruitment" (where names and photos are hidden to prevent bias)—are still optimized for specific formats or internal web forms that mimic the **Jagisogaeseo (Self-Introduction Letter)** structure.

Furthermore, the order of information matters. Did you know that many Korean firms still prefer a chronological order that feels "narrative"? They want to see the "Growth Process." If your resume skips straight to your latest job without providing the cultural or educational context of how you arrived there, you feel like a "floating" candidate with no roots.
