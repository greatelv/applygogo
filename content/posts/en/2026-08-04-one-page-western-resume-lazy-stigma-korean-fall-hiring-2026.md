## 1. The 'Jeongseong' Gap: Why "Less" is Actually "Least"

In the West, a resume is a highlight reel. In Korea, it is a testament of character. The concept of *Jeongseong* refers to the soul and devotion one puts into a task. When a Korean recruiter opens a file and sees a single page of bullet points, their first thought isn't "How professional!" It’s "Did this applicant even try?"

Korean applications traditionally involve the **Jagisogaeseo (Self-Introduction Letter)** and the **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo (Detailed Career Description)**. These documents aren't just lists; they are multi-layered narratives. If you aren't explaining your growth process, how you handled interpersonal conflict within your team, and why your personal philosophy aligns with the company’s "Founding Spirit," you are leaving 70% of the required information off the table.

## 2. Moving Beyond Results: The "How" and the "With Whom"

Western resumes are obsessed with **results**. *"Increased revenue by 20%," "Managed a team of 10," "Launched 3 products."*

In the 2026 Korean job market, results are the baseline—they are expected. What gets you the offer is the **process**. Korean HR managers are trained to look for **'Seongsil' (Sincerity/Diligence)**. They want to know:
*   **The 'How':** Did you achieve that 20% increase by working late, or by optimizing a process? (In Korea, the former often shows more character).
*   **The 'With Whom':** How did you interact with your seniors (Sunbae) and juniors (Hubae)? Did you foster 'Harmony' (Hwa)?

A standard Western bullet point: 
> *"Led cross-functional team to deliver project X on time."*

A 'Winning' Korean Career Description:
> *"As the lead of a 5-person cross-functional unit, I identified a communication bottleneck between design and engineering. By initiating daily 15-minute 'Tea-times' and documenting shared goals, I harmonized conflicting departmental interests, eventually leading to the successful delivery of Project X within the deadline, earning the 'Quarterly Excellence' recognition."*

See the difference? The second version shows your social intelligence and your willingness to put in the "invisible work" that Korean corporate culture thrives on.

## 3. The 2026 "Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo" Standard

By 2026, even "Blind Recruitment" (where age, photo, and school are sometimes hidden) has evolved. Companies now demand even more granular detail in the **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo**. They expect a chronological deep-dive that explains every gap and every transition.

If your resume starts with your most recent job and ends with your degree, you are missing the cultural nuance of **chronological storytelling**. In Korea, the "Growth Process" (Seongjang Gajeong) matters. They want to see the trajectory of your life, not just the highlights of your 30s.
