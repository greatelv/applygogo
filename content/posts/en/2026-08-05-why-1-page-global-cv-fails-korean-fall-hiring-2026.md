## 1. The Myth of the 1-Page Resume in Korea

In the West, brevity is a virtue. In Korea, detail is a sign of respect and "Seongsil" (sincerity/diligence). When a Korean recruiter opens your file and sees a single page of bullet points, their first thought isn't "Wow, look at this focused individual." Their first thought is "Why didn't this person put effort into our company's specific requirements?"

The Korean recruitment system is built on two pillars that the 1-page resume fails to support:
1. **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo (Detailed Experience Statement):** This is a multi-page document that breaks down every project, your specific role, the technologies used, and the quantifiable results.
2. **Jagisogaeseo (Self-Introduction Letter):** This is where you explain your "Growth Process," your "Strengths and Weaknesses," and your "Motive for Application."

If you haven't prepared these documents in the specific 2026 format, your application is effectively invisible.

## 2. The "Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo" – Why Bullet Points Fail

A standard English resume might say: *"Managed a team of 5 to increase sales by 20%."* 

In a Korean 'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo', this is insufficient. A Korean HR manager wants to see the **methodology of your persistence.** They want to know the specific interpersonal conflicts you resolved, the hierarchy you navigated, and the cultural adaptation you displayed. 

In 2026, the standard has shifted even further. With the rise of AI-driven filtering in companies like SK and LG, the keywords must be precisely aligned with the "Injaesang" (Ideal Candidate Image) of the company. A generic "Passionate Leader" tag won't cut it. You need to use terms like "Sajugwan" (Ownership) or "Hyeop-eop" (Collaboration) backed by specific, multi-paragraph examples that prove you can survive the intensity of the Korean corporate environment.

## 3. The "Jagisogaeseo" Trap: Translation is Not Localization

Many foreign applicants make the fatal mistake of writing their resume in English and using Google Translate or a basic translation service. 

**This is career suicide.**

Korean business language uses a complex system of honorifics (Jondaemal). If your resume uses the wrong level of formality, you come across as rude or uneducated. Furthermore, the "Growth Process" section (a staple of Korean resumes) often confuses Westerners. Korean companies aren't just looking for your skills; they are looking for your *character narrative*. They want to see how your childhood challenges shaped your current professional grit.
