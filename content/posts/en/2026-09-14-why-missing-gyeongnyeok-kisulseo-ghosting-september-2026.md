## 1. The Myth of the "One-Page Resume"

In the US, UK, and much of Europe, the "One-Page Rule" is king. If you can’t summarize your career in one page, you’re seen as unable to prioritize information. However, in the Korean corporate mindset, a one-page resume is often viewed as a mere "summary" or an "abstract." 

Korean HR managers don't just want to know *where* you worked; they want a granular, forensic breakdown of *how* you worked. This is where the **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo (Detailed Career Description)** comes in. Unlike the standard resume (Iryeokseo), which lists your history chronologically, the Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo is a separate, multi-page document that deep-dives into your technical contributions, project roles, and measurable outcomes.

Without this document, a Korean recruiter looking at your application feels like they are reading a book with every second chapter ripped out. They see the title, but they don't see the plot. In a pile of 1,000 applicants, "incomplete" data is the fastest way to the "Reject" pile.

## 2. Anatomy of a Winning Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo

What does a Korean recruiter actually look for in this document? It isn't just a list of responsibilities. It is a testament to your **'Seongsil' (Sincerity/Diligence)** and technical depth. A professional Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo must include:

*   **Project Period & Team Size:** Exactly how long did the project last? Were you leading 5 people or 50?
*   **Specific Role & Tech Stack:** Don't just say "Software Engineer." Say "Lead Backend Developer using Spring Boot, JPA, and AWS, responsible for architecting the microservices layer."
*   **Quantifiable Achievements:** "Improved efficiency" is a ghost word in Korea. "Reduced server latency by 15% through database indexing and query optimization" is a winning phrase.
*   **Key Challenges & Resolutions:** Korean culture highly values the process of overcoming hardship. Detailing a specific technical hurdle and how you solved it proves you have the grit to survive in a high-pressure Korean work environment.

If your current application only says "Responsible for project management," you are losing to a domestic candidate who has submitted a 3-page technical breakdown of every project they’ve touched since 2020.

## 3. The "Honorifics" Trap: Why Translation Isn't Enough

Many foreign applicants try to bypass the struggle by using Google Translate or ChatGPT to turn their English CV into Korean. This is a fatal mistake. 

The Korean language operates on a complex system of honorifics and business etiquette. Using the wrong verb ending (e.g., using "Banmal" or informal language instead of "Sipseunida" style) makes you look unprofessional, or worse, arrogant. Furthermore, industry-specific "Konglish" (Korean-style English) and native business terminology differ wildly.

A recruiter at a "Chaebol" (conglomerate) can smell a non-native, machine-translated resume from a mile away. It signals a lack of effort and a lack of cultural integration. If you can't bother to present your career in the local business language correctly, why would they believe you can navigate a 100% Korean-speaking office environment?
