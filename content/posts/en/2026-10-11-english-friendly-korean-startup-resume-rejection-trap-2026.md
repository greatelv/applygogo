## 1. The Myth of the "Thin" Resume

In the West, brevity is king. In Korea, brevity is often interpreted as a lack of sincerity. 

While a recruiter at a Google branch might want a quick 30-second scan of your bullet points, a Korean HR manager at a high-growth startup (like those in the Kakao or Coupang ecosystems) is looking for **'Professional Sincerity' (Jeonmunseong & Seongsil).** 

When you submit a 1-page PDF, the Korean decision-maker doesn’t see a "concise professional." They see an "abbreviated summary." They see someone who didn’t put in the effort to provide a detailed **'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo' (Experience Statement)**. In the competitive 2026 landscape, a Western CV looks functionally "thin" compared to the robust, multi-page dossiers submitted by local candidates and savvy international competitors.

## 2. The Decision-Maker is Still Korean

Even if your future manager is a Berkeley grad, the **hiring workflow** in Korean startups often requires approval from traditional HR departments or founders who value Korean corporate etiquette. 

These decision-makers are looking for specific structural cues:
- **The "Growth Process" (Seongjang Gwangjeong):** They want to know *how* you became the professional you are today, not just what you did last year.
- **Organizational Fit:** In Korea, skills are secondary to how you handle hierarchy and teamwork. A Western resume focuses on "I did X." A winning Korean resume focuses on "I contributed to the team's achievement of X."
- **The Honorifics Hurdle:** If you use Google Translate to turn your resume into Korean, you are committing career suicide. Subtle errors in honorifics (Jondaemal) signal that you haven't integrated into the culture—a massive red flag for "global" companies that still operate on Korean social foundations.

## 3. "Seongsil" (Sincerity) Over "Passion"

In Western CVs, we use buzzwords like "Passionate," "Self-starter," or "Disruptor." In the Korean context, these are fluff. 

The most valuable trait you can demonstrate is **Seongsil (Sincerity/Diligence).** How do you show this without just saying the word? You show it through **data-backed depth**. Instead of three bullet points for your last role, a Korean "Experience Statement" requires a breakdown of:
1. The project's background.
2. Your specific technical contribution.
3. The quantified organizational result.
4. What you learned/how you grew from the challenge.

Without these four pillars, your application lacks the "Professional Sincerity" required to be taken seriously by top-tier firms in 2026.
