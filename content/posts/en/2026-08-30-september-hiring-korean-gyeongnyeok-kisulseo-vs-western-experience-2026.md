## 1. The Trap of "Solo Heroics" vs. Technical Context

In the US or Europe, brevity is king. We are taught to use "action verbs" and "quantifiable results" to show how we were the hero of the story. However, the Korean corporate mindset—deeply rooted in organizational harmony and precise technical execution—finds this approach suspicious.

When a Korean HR manager at a company like Samsung reviews your "Experience" section, they aren't just looking for what you achieved; they are looking for **how you functioned within the system.**

Western bullets often look like this:
*   *“Optimized database performance by 30% through SQL query refactoring.”*

To a Korean recruiter, this is a skeleton without meat. They want to know:
*   What was the specific technical environment (Environment/Stack)?
*   What was the exact project scale (Budget/User base)?
*   What was your specific role within the hierarchy (Lead, Contributor, Support)?
*   What was the "before and after" narrative of the organizational pain point?

Without this context, your high-impact bullets feel like "empty boasting." In Korea, "Seongsil" (sincerity and diligence) is proven through the **depth of your technical description**, not the size of your adjectives.

## 2. The 'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo': The Document You Didn't Know You Needed

Most foreign applicants are familiar with the *Jagisogaeseo* (Self-introduction letter), but the real deal-breaker is the **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo (Detailed Career Description)**. 

While a Western resume is a summary, the Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo is a technical audit. In the Korean market, it is standard to provide a project-by-project breakdown. This includes:
1.  **Project Period**: Exact dates.
2.  **Tech Stack/Tools**: Every library, framework, and software used.
3.  **Key Achievements**: Detailed logic behind your decisions.
4.  **Learning/Growth**: How this project made you a better asset for the *next* company.

If you are only providing a standard 1-page resume, you are essentially bringing a knife to a gunfight. Korean candidates are submitting 3-4 pages of meticulous technical documentation. If yours is one page, the HR manager assumes you haven't done much—even if you have an MBA from an Ivy League school or years of experience at a FAANG company.

## 3. The Cultural Nuance: Why Translation is Your Enemy

Many applicants think the solution is simple: "I'll just translate my resume into Korean." 

**This is a fatal mistake.** 

A direct translation of a Western resume often creates "Translation Dissonance." The tone is usually too individualistic or uses incorrect honorifics (*Jondaemal*). For example, using the wrong level of humility when describing your role can make you seem arrogant or, conversely, unqualified.

Furthermore, Korean HR looks for specific keywords that indicate stability and organizational fit. Words like "Responsibility" (Chaegimgam), "Cooperation" (Hyeopryeok), and "Proactive Attitude" (Judojeok Taedo) must be woven into the technical descriptions. If you use a generic AI translator, these nuances are lost, and your resume "smells" like a non-native document within seconds.
