## 1. The "Invisible" Expertise: Why a 1-Page CV Fails in Korea

In the US or Europe, the 1-page resume is the gold standard. It’s a highlight reel designed for a 6-second scan. However, Korean HR culture operates on a completely different logic. 

In Korea, recruiters don't just want to know *where* you worked; they need to know exactly *where you fit* in the hierarchy. The traditional Korean "Resume" (Iryeokseo) is just a list of dates and titles. The **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo** is where the real evaluation happens.

Without this document, Korean HR managers cannot verify your seniority. They see "Project Manager" and, without a project-by-project breakdown of your specific role, contribution, and reporting line, they default to the safest, lowest-risk category: **Junior.**

## 2. The Anatomy of a 'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo'

To secure a senior-level position (Gyeongnyeok-jik) and the salary that comes with it, your documentation must bridge the gap between "what I did" and "how it fits the Korean corporate structure." A winning Career Statement in 2026 must include:

*   **Project Period & Hierarchy**: Not just the dates of employment, but the specific duration of every major project and your rank within that project team.
*   **Specific Role (R&R)**: Were you the 'Main' (PM) or a 'Support' (Participant)? In Korea, the distinction between *Gi-hoek* (Planning) and *Woon-young* (Operations) is massive. If you don't specify, they assume the latter.
*   **Quantifiable Tech Stack/Tools**: For tech roles, listing "Python" isn't enough. You must list exactly how Python was utilized in *Project A* versus *Project B*.
*   **Result (Performance)**: In Korean corporate culture, this is often expressed in percentages (e.g., "15% increase in efficiency") or specific awards/recognitions within the organization.

## 3. The "Translation" Trap: Why Google Translate is Career Suicide

Many applicants think, *"I'll just translate my English bullet points into Korean."* 

This is where most senior profiles die. Korean business language is governed by strict **honorifics (Jondaemal)** and industry-specific terminology that doesn't exist in English. A "Manager" in an American startup might be a "Team Jang" (Team Leader) or a "Gwa-jang" (Section Chief) depending on the company's size and structure.

If you use the wrong title or informal phrasing found in automated translation, you don't just look like a foreigner—you look **unprofessional.** Korean recruiters can smell a non-native, poorly localized resume from a mile away. It signals a lack of "culture fit" (K-Fit), which is often the #1 reason high-level foreign talent is rejected.
