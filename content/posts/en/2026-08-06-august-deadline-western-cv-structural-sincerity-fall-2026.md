## 1. The Cultural Gap: Why 'Impact' Isn't Enough

In London, New York, or Berlin, a resume is a highlight reel. You show the "what" and the "how much." In Korea, a resume—specifically the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* (Career Description Statement)—is a testament to your *Seongsil* (Sincerity/Diligence). 

Korean corporate culture is built on the foundation of process and documentation. When a recruiter sees a Western CV with three brief bullet points under a job title, their internal monologue isn't "Wow, this person is concise." It’s "Does this person actually respect the process of our company?" 

**Structural Sincerity** refers to the meticulous documentation of your career path. It’s not just about saying you increased sales by 20%. It’s about documenting the hierarchical structure of the project, your specific role within the team, the obstacles faced, and the professional growth that occurred. In Korea, the *process* validates the *result*.

## 2. The Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo: Moving Beyond Bullet Points

The biggest shock for Western applicants is the length and depth required. A standard Korean career description often spans 2 to 3 pages, even for junior roles. This is where the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* comes in.

Unlike the Western CV, which is chronological and brief, the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* requires:
- **Project Overviews**: A 2-3 sentence summary of every major project.
- **Specific Technical Contributions**: Not just "used Python," but "Optimized data pipelines using Python to reduce latency by 15% within a 5-person agile scrum."
- **Honorifics and Narrative**: Using professional, humble, yet confident Korean (*Gong-son-han* language) is non-negotiable.

If you are using Google Translate or ChatGPT to simply "translate" your English bullets into Korean, you are committing professional suicide. The nuance of honorifics (*Jondaemal*) in a professional context is a "shibboleth"—a way for recruiters to instantly identify who has truly integrated into the culture and who is just a "tourist" in the job market.
