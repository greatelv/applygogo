## 1. The Psychology of 'Seongsil-seong': More Than Just Skills

In the Korean corporate mindset, a resume is not just a record of what you *can* do; it is a testament to how much you *want* the job. Korean HR managers are looking for "Organizational Fit" (Sajik-jeok-hap-seong). They want to know if you are the type of person who will persist when things get difficult.

When a recruiter opens a concise, bullet-pointed English resume that has been hastily translated (or worse, left in English), they don't see "High Impact." They see an applicant who couldn't be bothered to adapt to the Korean format. They see a "low-effort" candidate.

In Korea, the document itself is your first "work task." If the document lacks depth, the recruiter assumes your work performance will also lack depth. This is the **Sincerity Gap**: the distance between your actual talent and the recruiter's perception of your dedication based on your paperwork.

## 2. Moving Beyond Bullets: The 'Context-Process-Contribution' Narrative

The Western "Action-Result" (e.g., "Increased sales by 20%") is necessary but insufficient in Korea. To bridge the Sincerity Gap, you must adopt a narrative structure that Korean managers recognize: **Context-Process-Contribution.**

*   **Context**: What was the specific organizational challenge? (In Korea, individual brilliance is secondary to team challenges).
*   **Process**: How did you interact with your colleagues? What specific "pains" did you endure to find the solution? This is where you demonstrate *Seongsil* (sincerity/hard work).
*   **Contribution**: How did this result help the *company* specifically, and how will you apply this "lesson learned" to the company you are currently applying to?

A "translated" bullet point fails because it strips away the "Process." Korean recruiters want to see the "Growth Process" (Seongjang-gwajeong)—a standard section in the *Jagisogaeseo* (Self-Introduction Letter). If you omit the story of your struggle and growth, you are essentially submitting an incomplete exam.

## 3. The "Native" Trap: Why Google Translate is Career Suicide

I’ve reviewed thousands of resumes for candidates targeting Samsung and Hyundai. The quickest way to the "Reject" pile is a resume that "smells" like a machine translation. 

Korean is a language of nuances, hierarchy, and honorifics. Using the wrong level of politeness (*Jondaemal*) in your *Jagisogaeseo* signals a lack of cultural awareness that is instant disqualification for any role that requires internal communication. Furthermore, the formatting expectations—reverse chronological education starting from high school, specific photo standards, and the dreaded .HWP file format—create a barrier of entry that most global talents fail to scale.
