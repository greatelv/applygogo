## 1. The Fatal Flaw: One Page is Not Enough

In Western recruitment, the one-page rule is king. If you can’t summarize your career in one page, you’re seen as unable to prioritize. 

In Korea, the logic is inverted. A one-page resume for a mid-career professional suggests a lack of depth, a lack of sincerity, and—most dangerously—a lack of actual involvement in the projects listed. Korean HR managers are looking for the **'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo' (Detailed Work Description)**. 

While your Western CV might say:
*   *"Led a team of 5 to increase revenue by 20% over Q3."*

A Korean recruiter wants to know:
*   Which specific departments did you coordinate with?
*   What was the exact software architecture used?
*   How did you handle the conflict when the budget was cut mid-project?
*   What was your specific role in the 'Hyeop-eop' (collaboration) process?

If you simply translate your English bullet points into Korean, you are leaving 70% of your value off the table. To a Korean manager, your experience looks like a skeleton without the meat.

## 2. The "How" and "With Whom" vs. The "What"

Western resumes are result-oriented. Korean resumes are **process-oriented**. 

In the Korean corporate mindset, a result can be a fluke, but a well-executed process is repeatable. When a recruiter at a *Chaebol* (conglomerate) reviews your 'thin' global resume, they see the "What" (the 20% increase) but they don't see the "How" (the methodology) or the "With Whom" (the organizational fit).

In Korea, the concept of **'Inseong' (Character/Integrity)** and **'Seongsil' (Sincerity)** are evaluated through the level of detail in your writing. If you provide a dense, structurally sound description of your workflow, you are demonstrating your diligence. If you provide three bullet points, you are perceived as someone who does the bare minimum.

## 3. The Language Barrier is Not Just About Vocabulary

Most global applicants make the mistake of using Google Translate or a generic AI to flip their English CV into Korean. This is a fast track to the "Rejected" pile. 

Korean business language relies heavily on **honorifics (Jondatmal)** and specific "HR-speak" that denotes seniority and professional respect. A direct translation often sounds like a child speaking or, worse, someone who is being unintentionally arrogant. 

Furthermore, the formatting of a Korean resume—often required in HWP or specific PDF structures—is a logistical nightmare for those used to LinkedIn-style exports. You are expected to list your education from high school onwards, include certifications with specific issuing numbers, and often provide a 'Jagisogaeseo' (Personal Statement) that follows a four-pillar narrative: Growth Process, Personality Pros/Cons, Motivation for Application, and Post-hiring Aspirations.
