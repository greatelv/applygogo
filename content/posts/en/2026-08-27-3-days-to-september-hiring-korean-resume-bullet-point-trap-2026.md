## 1. The "Sincerity Gap": Why Bullet Points Fail in Korea

In North America or Europe, recruiters spend six seconds on a resume. They want the highlights. In Korea, the resume is considered a formal document that reflects your personality, your respect for the company, and your ability to document processes.

When a Korean HR manager at a conglomerate like SK or LG opens a file and sees a series of abbreviated bullet points, their first thought isn't "Wow, look at those achievements." Their first thought is: **"Why didn't this person explain the process?"**

In the Korean context, *how* you achieved a result is often more important than the result itself. They are looking for "team harmony" (*Inhwa*) and "perseverance" (*Noryeok*). A bullet point hides the struggle, the collaboration, and the structural steps you took. To bridge this gap, you must move away from "Managed a team of 5" and toward a narrative that explains the hierarchy, the challenges faced by the team, and the specific methodology you used to lead them.

## 2. 'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo' vs. The Western CV

If you are applying for a mid-to-senior level position, the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* (Career Description) is your most important document. Unlike a CV, which is a summary, the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* is an evidentiary narrative.

Here is what Korean HR expects to see in this document:
- **Specific Project Breakdown:** Not just "Software Development," but the specific tech stack, the duration of the project, and your exact role within the organization.
- **Problem-Solving Context:** What was the crisis? How did you align your actions with the company’s goals?
- **Quantifiable Data + Qualitative Context:** You must back your data with *why* it mattered to the organization’s long-term strategy.

Using generic English-to-Korean translation services often fails here because they don't account for **honorifics (Jondaemal)**. Using the wrong level of politeness in your resume is the fastest way to get your application deleted. In Korea, professional writing requires a specific tone that balances confidence with humility—a nuance that Google Translate cannot capture.

## 3. The "Jagisogaeseo" (Self-Introduction) Hurdle

Beyond the resume lies the *Jagisogaeseo*. This isn't a cover letter; it’s an essay. Korean companies will ask you about your "Growth Process" or your "Pros and Cons." 

Foreign candidates often make the mistake of being too individualistic here. They focus on their "personal brand." In Korea, you need to show how your personal history has prepared you to be a dedicated "member of the hive." You need to demonstrate *Sincerity* (Seongsil). 

If you don't mention how you overcame a hardship through group effort or how your values align with the specific "Founder's Philosophy" of the company (e.g., Samsung’s "Injae-jeil"), you are missing the mark.
