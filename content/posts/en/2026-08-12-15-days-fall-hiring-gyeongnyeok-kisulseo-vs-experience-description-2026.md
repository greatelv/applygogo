## 1. The 'Experience' vs. 'Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo' Gap

In a Western resume, you are taught to be concise. "Increased sales by 20% using X strategy" is the gold standard. You use bullet points to save the recruiter's time. 

In Korea, the philosophy is different. A Korean HR manager at a company like **Coupang** or **Naver** isn't just looking for what you did; they are looking for **how** you did it and **with whom** you collaborated. They want to see the "process" (Gwa-jeong).

The **Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo** is a standalone document (or a massive section) that goes deep into the technical weeds. While your *Iryeokseo* (General Resume) is the "What," the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* is the "How." If you only provide three bullet points per job, the recruiter assumes you didn't do much or, worse, that you are too lazy to explain your value properly.

## 2. The Three Pillars of a Winning 'Kisulseo'

To turn a rejection into an offer, your experience must be restructured into these three Korean corporate pillars:

### A. The Project Context (Background)
Don't just name the company. Explain the scale of the project. Was it a 1-billion KRW project? How many team members were involved? In Korea, hierarchy and team structure matter. Explicitly stating your role within the hierarchy (e.g., "Main Lead of a 5-person cross-functional team") provides the cultural context the recruiter needs.

### B. The Methodology (The 'How')
In the West, we focus on results. In Korea, we focus on the **specific tools and communication methods**. Did you use Jira? How did you handle conflicts with the development team? A "Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo" should detail the specific hurdles you faced and the logical steps you took to overcome them. This proves your *Seongsil* (sincerity/diligence).

### C. The Quantified Contribution (The 'Result')
Finally, the data. But unlike Western resumes, this data must be tied back to the company’s bottom line or organizational growth. "Improved efficiency" is too vague. "Reduced operational costs by 15% during the Q3 peak season, contributing to the department's 'A' grade evaluation" is how you speak the language of a Korean executive.

## 3. Why Google Translate is Your Career's Worst Enemy

Many applicants think they can just write their "Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo" in English and run it through an AI translator. This is a fatal mistake. 

Korean business language relies heavily on **honorifics (Jondaemal)** and specific corporate terminology that doesn't have a direct 1:1 translation. Using the wrong level of formality in your experience statement suggests you don't understand Korean office culture (*Nunchi*). If you can't describe your own career with the correct professional nuances, the recruiter will assume you'll struggle in a high-stakes meeting at a *Chaebol*.
