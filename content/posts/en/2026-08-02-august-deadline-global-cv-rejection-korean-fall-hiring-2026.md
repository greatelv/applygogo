## 1. The "Impact" vs. "Sincerity" (Jeongseong) Divide

In the West, we are taught to highlight "Solo Achievements." *I increased revenue by 20%. I led a team of ten.* 

In the Korean corporate psyche, these statements are often met with skepticism. HR managers aren't just looking for what you did; they are looking for **how you did it within a hierarchy.** They are looking for *Jeongseong* (sincerity/devotion) and *Seongsil* (integrity). 

A Western resume often lacks the **"Growth Process" (Seongjang Gwajeong)** narrative that remains a cornerstone of the Korean *Jagisogaeseo* (Self-Introduction Letter). Korean recruiters want to see the struggle, the adaptation, and the ultimate alignment with the company's "Injaesang" (ideal talent image). If your resume only lists results without the narrative of organizational harmony, it will be discarded as "too individualistic."

## 2. The "Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo" Trap

Many foreigners make the mistake of thinking a "Experience Statement" (*Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo*) is just a longer version of a resume. It is not. 

In Korea, the *Gyeongnyeok Kisulseo* is a technical document that requires a specific structure:
1.  **Project Period & Role**
2.  **Core Technical Stack/Methodology**
3.  **Specific Contribution (with proof of teamwork)**
4.  **Key Learning Point (How this made you a better 'Company Man')**

Global experts often fail because they provide a "flat" narrative. They use active verbs like "Spearheaded," which can sometimes sound overly aggressive in a culture that values "Collaborative Leadership." You need to translate your "American-style" dominance into "Korean-style" expertise.

## 3. Why Google Translate is a Career Killer

I have seen thousands of resumes where the applicant used AI or Google Translate to convert their English CV into Korean. This is the fastest way to get blacklisted.

Korean is a language of **honorifics and social hierarchy.** Using the wrong verb ending (e.g., using *Banmal* or informal polite instead of the professional *Hapsyo-che*) suggests to an HR manager that you will be a communication liability in the office. They see a "translated" resume and immediately think: *"This person cannot handle a client meeting in Seoul."*

Your resume shouldn't just be translated; it needs to be **transcreated.** Your English achievements must be mapped to the specific cultural keywords that resonate with a Korean hiring committee—keywords like *Responsibility* (Chaegimgam), *Proactiveness* (Judojeok), and *Communication* (Sotong).
